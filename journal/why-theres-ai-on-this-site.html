Why There's AI All Over This Site (and What That Actually Costs)

I'm not a developer. I don't know how to code, and until I started this I'd never built a website in my life. This site exists because I had the idea, the standards, and the stubbornness to keep saying "no, that's not right, try again", and because I used AI to actually build it. Claude wrote the code. Claude fixed the bugs. Some of the photographs on this site, including the rabbit on the homepage, were generated by AI rather than photographed by a person. I signed up for the accounts, made the decisions, and pushed back constantly when something didn't look or feel right, but the technical work was done by AI, start to finish.

I'm telling you that plainly because I'd rather you heard it from me than noticed it yourself and wondered why I hadn't said anything. And because I know AI is a genuinely sore subject for a lot of people who care about the same things I care about here, it uses real resources, it's trained in ways a lot of artists and writers never agreed to, and "I used a chatbot to build my website" can sound, at best, a bit lazy.

So I want to actually sit with that, rather than wave it away.

What AI actually costs. This isn't nothing. Every question I've asked Claude while building this site used real electricity and real water, somewhere in a data centre. A single query to a large AI model can use less than half a watt-hour on the smaller, cheaper end, or well over 30 watt-hours on the largest, most heavy-duty models handling long, complex requests, alongside a water footprint, from cooling those data centres, that researchers have measured in the tens to low hundreds of millilitres per query on the more demanding end. One study projects that a single widely-used model, at its current usage scale, could account for over a million kilolitres of water a year, roughly what 1.2 million people drink annually.1 Scaled up across everyone using these tools, worldwide, constantly, that adds up to something real. I'm not going to pretend it doesn't.

What I'm weighing it against. Here's the thing I keep coming back to, though. This site exists to do one job: help people avoid paying companies to hurt animals, and to make it slightly easier to live a bit closer to vegan without feeling like it's a huge sacrifice. So it feels fair to actually look at what sits on the other side of the scale.

Animal testing. At least 192.1 million animals were used in experiments worldwide in a single year, on the most recent reliable global count anyone's produced.2 That's animals bred, kept, fed, and disposed of specifically to be tested on, at a scale most people never think about because it happens behind closed doors. I couldn't find a solid, sourced figure for the water, energy or land footprint of that industry specifically, it doesn't appear to be measured and published the way AI's footprint now is, which is itself worth noticing. The absence of a number isn't evidence the impact is small. It's evidence nobody's been made to publish one.

Animal agriculture, which is really what most of this site is actually about, since testing policy is only one half of "cruelty-free", the other half is what happens to animals raised for food, leather and fur, milk and eggs. And here the numbers are not in short supply. Around 83 billion land animals are slaughtered for meat globally every year.3 Producing a single kilogram of beef takes roughly 15,000 litres of water once you count what the animal drank, what was used to clean and process it, and what it took to grow its feed, against roughly 300 litres for a kilogram of vegetables.4 Livestock uses close to 80% of the world's agricultural land while providing under 20% of the world's calories.5

So: a chatbot session costs a bottle of water and a lightbulb's worth of electricity. A kilogram of beef costs a bathtub and a half.

I'm not saying that to excuse AI's footprint, or to say "well, it could be worse." I'm saying it because if I'm going to hold companies accountable for the resources their choices cost, animal and environmental alike, I should be honest about the resources mine cost too, rather than pretending building this site was free.

Where I land. I used AI to build this because I couldn't have built it otherwise, not because it was the effortless option. Every page on here went through several rounds of "no, that's wrong, do it again," which is its own kind of cost in time and, yes, in more queries than a first-time-right build would have taken. I don't think that makes the site's purpose less worthwhile. I do think it means I owe you honesty about how it was made, rather than a polished story that leaves that part out.

If you'd rather support sites and shops that don't use AI at all, that's a completely reasonable line to draw, and I won't pretend this one clears it. If what matters more to you is whether the information here is accurate and the animals it's trying to help are real, I hope this is useful regardless of how it got built.

A note on the numbers above: I've linked every source I used. Where I couldn't find a solid figure, animal testing's own environmental footprint, specifically, I've said so rather than guessing or rounding up.

  1. "How Hungry is AI? Benchmarking Energy, Water, and Carbon Footprint of LLM Inference", a 2025 study benchmarking real models.
  2. Cruelty Free International, "Facts and Figures", citing 2015 data, described by the organisation as "the world's most reliable global figure to date," which tells you something about how hard this is to even measure.
  3. UN FAO, via Our World in Data, the same rate this site's own "land animals killed for meat" counter runs on.
  4. Mekonnen & Hoekstra, Water Footprint Network, a widely-cited academic water footprint study.
  5. Poore & Nemecek, 2018, via Our World in Data.
