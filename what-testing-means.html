what "tested on animals" actually means

Years ago I saw a meme of a gorilla wearing lipstick. The caption was something about animal testing. I laughed at it.

I didn't know then that it wasn't a joke about a gorilla in lipstick, because that isn't what happens. Nobody is dabbing blusher on a chimp and taking notes. What actually happens is much quieter, much more clinical, and much worse.

So here's the plain version. No photographs, no shock tactics. Just what the words on the label are covering up.

the tests themselves

The Draize test is the one most people have heard of without knowing the name. A substance is placed directly onto the eye of a conscious rabbit. The rabbit is restrained so it can't move; its eyelids are held open so it can't blink the substance away. It's then observed for up to fourteen days and scored for redness, swelling, ulceration, bleeding and, in some cases, blindness. Often without pain relief.

Rabbits are used for this partly because they produce very few tears, so whatever is put in their eye stays there. Albino rabbits specifically, because there's no pigment to obscure the damage, which makes it easier to score.

There's a skin version too. The fur is shaved off, the substance goes on, and the resulting reaction is measured.

Lethal dose testing does what the name says. Animals are forced to swallow, inhale or be injected with a substance to find the dose at which half of them die. The measurement is the death rate.

Longer studies test for things like cancer, genetic damage, and harm to developing foetuses. A single carcinogenicity study can use around 400 animals.

Almost all of them are killed at the end, so their organs can be examined. That isn't a failure of the process. It's the process.

why beagles

This is the part I find hardest.

Of all the non-human animals in this story, dogs are the ones people tend to flinch at hardest, maybe because we already know their personalities. Beagles are the most-used dog in laboratories worldwide, and it isn't because of their biology. It's because of their temperament. They're friendly. They're trusting. They're bred to work in packs, so they don't resist. They forgive you.

A lab technician quoted by the Beagle Freedom Project put it plainly: they won't fight back, they let us do anything we want to them, that's why we like beagles.

The exact trait that makes them good family dogs is the reason they're chosen. Around 4,000 dogs a year are used in UK laboratories, the overwhelming majority of them beagles. Very few are ever rehomed.

"but it's banned here"

Partly. Cosmetic testing is banned in the UK and EU, and several other countries have followed. That's real progress and worth saying out loud.

But: ingredients can still be tested elsewhere and used here. Companies selling into markets that legally require animal testing can still be paying for it while calling themselves cruelty-free at home. Household products, chemicals and pharmaceuticals sit under entirely different rules. And a ban on paper doesn't retire the labs, it moves them.

That's why certification matters more than a bunny drawn on a bottle.

the scale of it, in the UK

Numbers can't do what a Draize test description does, but they're worth having, because they stop this being an abstract argument. According to the UK Home Office's own Statistics of Scientific Procedures on Living Animals, Great Britain, 2025, there were 2.54 million procedures on animals in Great Britain in 2025, down from 2.64 million the year before.

That 2.54 million splits into two quite different categories. 1.32 million (52%) were "experimental procedures" — an animal used in an actual test or study. The other 1.22 million (48%) were "creation and breeding of genetically altered animals" — a separate category covering animals bred with a specific genetic modification, not animals used in a test for a specific experiment that day.

Of the 1.32 million experimental procedures specifically, the species breakdown was: mice 56%, fish 16%, birds 10%, rats 9%, specially protected species (dogs, cats, horses, non-human primates) 1%, and other species 8%. That breakdown applies only to the experimental-procedures figure — not to the full 2.54 million total, which also includes breeding.

One more distinction worth holding onto: "procedures" isn't the same as "animals." A single animal can be counted more than once if it's used in multiple procedures, so the true number of individual animals behind these figures is somewhat lower than the procedure count above — the Home Office data doesn't give a single clean "total animals" figure, so we won't invent one here.
